    Commissioners approve solar farm reinvestment zone despite opposition

    • BY SHANE MONACO | TELEGRAM STAFF
    • Nov 6, 2023
    • Nov 6, 2023
    • Facebook
    • Twitter
    • WhatsApp
    • SMS
    • Email
    • Facebook
    • Twitter
    • WhatsApp
    • SMS
    • Email
    • Print
    • Save

    Despite pushback from local residents, the reinvestment zone for a new solar farm in Bell County was approved Monday.

    The Bell County Gransolar Texas Twenty Reinvestment Zone No. 1 was approved 4-1 by the Commissioners Court at its regular meeting with Commissioner Russell Schneider voting against. The approval of the reinvestment zone lays the foundation for the county to give the solar project a tax break as part of a later agreement.

    The proposed solar farm would be located north of FM 2670 between Maxdale and Pecan Creek roads. The site is near Central Texas State Veterans Cemetery in Killeen and north of the Lampasas River in the county’s Precinct 4.

    While the county has faced opposition to other solar projects, Commissioner Louie Minor continued to tell residents that setting up these reinvestment zones and giving tax breaks were the county’s only means to control these projects.

    “That is why many people live in the county so the government cannot tell you what to do,” Minor said. “The county doesn’t have land use authority like the city does to designate commercial land, agricultural land and single family housing. So we are kind of stuck in a position where we can listen to your concerns and put them into a tax abatement agreement.”

    Officials said the proposed solar farm is expected to span approximately 800 acres and cost the company $160 million to build.

    Construction, if approved to move forward, is expected to start in the second quarter of 2025 and be completed in the third quarter of 2026.

    Once completed, officials said the solar farm will generate 150 megawatts of electricity. One megawatt equates to 1 million watts and can power between 150 and 210 homes at once.

    Resident comments

    During the Monday meeting, more than a dozen local residents from around the county, including those bordering the project, came to speak out against the project.

    Temple resident John Asbury said he understood the need for solar panels, especially in Texas, but wanted to make sure that the land underneath the panels was preserved as well.

    “I would suggest that, in the tax abatement, all these solar panel companies be required to place that land back as prairie grasses,” Asbury said.

    Another resident who spoke was Alfred Smith, who lives near the planned solar farm. Smith said he was concerned about many aspects of the project including any reflected light.

    Smith pointed out that many of those who own homes near the project are elderly and retired, limiting their options when it comes to dealing with the solar farm.

    “I’m 83 and we cannot hop up and sell our home at a 50% to 60% loss and try and move, we are just not capable of it anymore and we are not young,” Smith said. “We will leave our homes as our legacy to our kids, but with this we have hardly anything for them because we are going to take on humongous loss.”

    Petra Clarke, who also lives near the proposed solar farm, said she was concerned about any possible health effects that could come from the panels.

    One key concern for Clarke was any electromagnetic frequencies emitted by the solar panels or the associated electrical components used to put the power on the electrical grid. While Clarke cited statistics from the World Health Organization on the effects of these frequencies on people, the organization has also stated that low levels, such as those emitted by solar panels, are not shown to be harmful to humans.

    Other residents who spoke against the project cited concerns about the noise the site could produce, with one resident playing a recording that sounded like a high screech.

    Schneider opposed

    The prospect of residents hearing that constant noise was what made Schneider vote against the reinvestment zone.

    Schneider said he had not liked the solar projects coming to the county but had believed that it was for the best to work with the companies through these tax abatements.

    Despite his original thoughts, Schneider said the public hearing changed his mind on helping these companies with these projects.

    “I would tell you that I hate telling anyone what they can do with their own land, whether it be a hog farm, solar or whatever,” Schneider said. “But, I have gone out there and it is devastating. It is acres and acres and acres.”

    Schneider also said that even if the Commissioners didn’t approve the tax abatement, it might not matter in the end.

    “Can we disallow them to be there? No,” Schneider said. “But we don’t have to help them. Just that little bit may be enough, I am not sure. If not, then it is not.”

    Commissioner Bill Schumann, a retired engineer for Wilsonart International, said that the sound played by the resident was likely an inverter and was not normal for that part. He said the part on the recorded solar panel should have been replaced.

    Schumann, who has dealt with many similar projects on the east side of the county, said he also didn’t necessarily like solar projects but knew that the county needed to approve these tax cuts in order to have any say in regulating them.

    “I hope everyone understands that we are not proponents of this and we don’t enlist these people and ask them to come here,” Schumann said. “When they do, I think we have an opportunity and a responsibility to do everything we can to protect adjacent property owners. We are not trying to protect the people trying to put solar farms in.”

    Officials said the earliest a tax abatement could come before the Commissioners Court would be the body’s Dec. 11 meeting, but that would require the solar company to move quickly.
